Research

We build human tissues on chips and the tools to measure them, then use both to ask how microbes and metabolism shape disease.

Microbes and colorectal cancer

The colon is lined by epithelium that lives in constant contact with bacteria and the molecules they make. We build colon organoid models and microfluidic chips in which living bacteria and human epithelium share the same space, so their interactions can be controlled and measured.

Current work asks how bile salts, bacterial enzymes such as bile salt hydrolase, and other microbial metabolites influence bacterial colonization, host signaling and tumor behavior in colorectal cancer organoids.

Liver–fat crosstalk

Fat tissue and the liver signal to each other constantly, and that conversation goes wrong in insulin resistance and fatty liver disease. We link adipose and liver tissues on chip to study it with human and primary cells under flow.

This builds on our long-running work on keeping hepatocytes and adipocytes functional in microfluidic culture, including liver sinusoid models and adipose tissue-chips.

Microbiome and drug response

Gut bacteria change how drugs are absorbed and metabolized. Many of these bacteria need low oxygen, so we built intestine chips with local control of oxygen tension that let anaerobic conditions and human cells coexist.

We pair these experiments with pharmacokinetic models to understand how bacteria alter drug exposure, for example for tacrolimus and sulfasalazine.

Sensing, control and computation

Living systems change continuously, so we develop ways to measure them continuously: microfluidic assays for secreted proteins and cholesterol, electrochemical sensors, and analysis of volatile compounds in breath and other samples.

We combine these measurements with mathematical models, machine learning and closed-loop control of cell cultures.
